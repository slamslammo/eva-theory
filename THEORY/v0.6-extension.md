## Preface

This document extends the v0.5 framework. It does not replace v0.5. The Claim A and Claim B of v0.5, the C1–C3 operating conditions, the L1–L3 architectural core, the four engineering contributions, and the L4/L5 weaker derivations are all carried forward unchanged.

What v0.6 adds is the part of EVA that v0.5 was able to leave implicit because v0.5's reference implementation operated in a single, narrow class of environment — one in which the agent's own runtime substrate was effectively the entire world it had to maintain. In that setting, several questions did not need explicit answers: what counts as the persistence target, what distinguishes designer-given capability from learned capability, what an outcome looks like when it has multiple incommensurable dimensions, and what observable form continuous existence takes such that the architecture can be evaluated from outside its own assumptions.

When EVA is asked to organize an agent in any environment that has metabolic cost, external resources, environmental risk, or time horizons longer than a single bounded task, these questions stop being optional. v0.6 makes them explicit, as theoretical commitments rather than implementation choices. Specific environments, specific implementations, and specific validation experiments are deliberately not part of this document. They are matters for the engineering and validation work that this theoretical framework will subsequently support.

The chapters are presented as a self-contained extension. References to v0.5 sections preserve v0.5's section numbering. Appendix R records the specific revisions v0.6 makes to interpretations of v0.5 passages; v0.5 itself is preserved unchanged.

## 1. Active Persistence

### 1.1 Two readings of continuous existence

v0.5 introduced continuous existence as a first-order constraint. The phrase admits two readings, which v0.5 did not need to distinguish but which v0.6 must.

The **passive reading** holds that an agent committed to continuous existence should preserve its current state. State values should not degrade. Conditions should not deteriorate. The agent succeeds at continuous existence by ensuring nothing gets worse.

The **active reading** holds that an agent committed to continuous existence should preserve its capacity to continue existing. The agent's projected ability to act, recover, and persist over a forward time horizon is the protected quantity. Current state values may degrade if doing so increases the agent's projected future capacity to exist; current state values may be defended if doing so increases that capacity. State preservation and persistence are no longer the same thing.

The two readings are nearly indistinguishable in environments where current state and future capacity move together. In any environment where the two can come apart — where preserving current state can damage future capacity, or where damaging current state can improve future capacity — the readings prescribe different behavior, different drive structures, different evaluation criteria.

EVA's commitment is to the active reading. The reasons are structural, not preferential.

### 1.2 The cost of inaction

The passive reading carries a hidden assumption: that inaction is a neutral baseline. If state values are not degrading, the agent need not act; if they are degrading, the agent should act to restore them. Inaction has zero cost when nothing is changing.

This assumption holds in environments without metabolic processes, without environmental dynamics, without competitors, and without expiring opportunities. It does not hold in environments where any of these are present. In an environment with metabolic cost, the agent's resources decline whether or not it acts. In an environment with active threats, danger accumulates over time when not addressed. In an environment with limited windows, opportunities pass.

When inaction has cost, the passive reading produces a behavior pattern of slow, invisible decline. The agent does not act because no immediate threat is visible; meanwhile, background processes consume the resources, close the windows, and increase the exposure that determine future capacity. The agent appears stable until it is suddenly not. There is no warning, because the warning would have required the agent to be reasoning about the trajectory of its situation under inaction, which the passive reading does not require.

The active reading treats inaction as one option among many, with its own cost trajectory. To evaluate inaction against alternative actions, the agent must reason not only about current state but about projected state under each option, including the option of doing nothing. This is the structural reason rate sensing — the awareness of how state values are changing, not only what they are — must be part of L1 in any environment where the active reading is to hold.

#### 1.2.1 What rate sensing is

Rate sensing is the structured observation of **how a dimension's value is changing over time**, in addition to what the value currently is.

For any dimension that participates in L1 judgment, rate sensing answers three questions:

- **Direction**: is the value improving, stable, or degrading?
- **Magnitude**: how fast is it changing relative to typical variation in this dimension?
- **Acceleration**: is the rate of change itself increasing, holding, or decreasing?

A dimension can be in a healthy state but with a sharply degrading rate, indicating that intervention should happen before the state itself becomes unhealthy. A dimension can be in a degraded state with an improving rate, indicating that intervention is in progress and may not need additional action.

#### 1.2.2 Which dimensions must support rate sensing

Dimensions are classified at the framework level into four rate-sensing tiers:

| Tier | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `required` | Dimensions whose threshold crossing constitutes a failure event for an active persistence target. Rate sensing **must** be implemented. |
| `recommended` | Dimensions that affect future viability but do not directly constitute failure. Rate sensing **should** be implemented; absence requires recorded rationale. |
| `optional` | Background context dimensions. Rate sensing may be implemented if useful. |
| `unsupported_with_reason` | Dimensions where rate sensing is currently not available. The reason must be recorded in the dimension's specification (data sparsity, inherent stochasticity, semantic incompatibility). |

This tiering prevents two failure modes: dimensions that should have rate sensing being silently treated as static, and dimensions that should be static being forced into spurious rate computation that produces noisy or meaningless trajectories.

The tier classification is part of each dimension's specification, declared at the scenario level at registration time. The framework audits the classification and flags inconsistencies — a dimension participating in a failure condition but not in the `required` tier, for instance.

#### 1.2.3 How rate is computed

The mechanisms in this subsection are recommended implementation profiles, not the core theoretical commitment. The theoretical commitment is that rate sensing must represent how a relevant dimension is changing over time, including direction, magnitude, and, where meaningful, acceleration. Window length, sample interval, aggregation method, and decay parameters are engineering specifications declared per dimension.

The framework specifies that rate sensing maintains a **sliding window** of recent values for each supported dimension. The window is parameterized per dimension by:

- **Window length**: how many recent values are retained
- **Sample interval**: how often new values are added to the window
- **Aggregation method**: how the window produces the current rate estimate

Three aggregation methods are recognized:

- **Linear regression** over the window, producing a slope. Robust to outliers within the window, but assumes near-linear change over the window length.
- **Exponentially weighted moving average** of pairwise differences, with a decay parameter controlling how quickly old observations lose weight. Faster to respond to recent changes than linear regression.
- **First-order difference** between current and one-window-back, ignoring intermediate values. Cheapest, but loses information about within-window dynamics.

The recommended default is exponentially weighted moving average. Specific dimensions may declare a different method appropriate to their dynamics.

The framework does not fix exact window lengths or decay parameters; these are dimension-specific configuration. What the framework fixes is that the method must be declared per dimension and that the rate output must include both a direction (improving / stable / degrading) and a magnitude estimate.

#### 1.2.4 How rate combines with status in judgment

The judgment layer produces a per-dimension assessment carrying `status`, `evidence`, and `rate_context`, structured as:

- `available`: whether rate sensing produced a usable estimate
- `direction`: improving / stable / degrading / unknown
- `magnitude`: a normalized score or categorical label
- `acceleration`: whether the rate is itself changing

The combination rules:

- **Status sets baseline pressure**. The current status of a dimension determines the baseline pressure level associated with it. Healthy status produces no baseline pressure; degraded produces moderate; critical produces high.
- **Rate modulates pressure urgency**. A degraded dimension with a degrading rate produces higher-urgency pressure than the same status with an improving rate. The modulation operates on the urgency dimension of pressure, not on whether the pressure exists.
- **Rate can produce anticipatory pressure** when configured. For dimensions in the `required` rate-sensing tier with a configured anticipatory threshold, a sufficiently fast rate of degradation produces pressure even when current status is still healthy. The threshold for anticipatory pressure is dimension configuration; this is not a default-on mechanism.

The distinction matters: status without rate gives the agent a reactive system that responds when things are already bad. Status with rate-modulated urgency and configured anticipatory pressure gives the agent the forward-looking responsiveness that active persistence requires.

#### 1.2.5 Fallback when rate is unavailable

Rate sensing requires history. At certain moments, history is absent or insufficient:

- The first sensing cycle after an existence field activates
- After an episode reset (in fields with episode boundaries)
- After a sensor outage that left the window stale
- For dimensions that have just been activated

The framework specifies the fallback behavior:

- `rate_context.available` is `False`
- `rate_context.direction` is `unknown`
- `rate_context.magnitude` is `None`
- `rate_context.acceleration` is `None`

Downstream consumers must treat unknown direction as **neither positive nor negative evidence**. They must not interpret it as "stable" — the absence of information is not the same as the presence of stability information.

The framework records when rate is unavailable so that diagnostic analysis can distinguish "agent saw the situation stabilize" from "agent had no information about whether the situation was stabilizing."

### 1.3 Future viability as primary evaluative object

If the agent is preserving its capacity to continue existing rather than its current state, what exactly is being preserved?

The answer is what we will call **future viability**: the agent's projected capacity, over a forward time horizon, to continue existing, to act effectively, to recover from disturbance, and to acquire what it needs in order to do these things. Future viability is the **primary evaluative object** of the architecture, not a scalar utility target. The architecture does not maximize future viability against an unbounded objective function; it maintains and expands future viability under the structural constraints established in subsequent chapters.

This distinction matters. An agent that maximized any single objective without bound would, by the standard arguments around instrumental convergence, sacrifice properties of itself that constitute its identity in pursuit of that objective. EVA's commitment is to bounded viability ordering: the agent prefers states with higher projected future viability, but the preference operates within a fixed structural envelope, not as an unconstrained optimizer.

Future viability is not a single number. It is a vector with at least the following components:

- The probability that the agent does not enter an unrecoverable state in the near term
- The adequacy of the agent's resource base for sustaining action
- The breadth and depth of the capabilities the agent can draw on
- The agent's expected speed and reliability of recovery from disturbance
- The agent's potential for acquiring new capability before it is needed

These components are partially independent and partially ordered. A high score in resource adequacy does not compensate for a critical failure in immediate survival. A strong capability inventory does not compensate for the loss of recovery capacity. The components are also not all relevant in all environments — some may be inactive or trivially constant in environments that do not support them — but where they are relevant, they are not interchangeable.

Future viability becomes the substrate over which evaluation in L3 takes place. Candidate actions are scored not by their effect on the current state vector but by their effect on the projected future viability vector. An action that reduces a current state value but improves projected future viability is a viability gain. An action that maintains current state but exposes the agent to unacceptable risk in the projected future is a viability loss, regardless of how stable the current state appears.

The structural continuity of the agent itself — the preservation of the properties that make the agent recognizably itself — is not a component of future viability. It is a structural invariant (chapter 4) that bounds how viability is pursued. Future viability is what the agent maintains and expands; structural continuity is what the maintenance must not violate.

### 1.4 Bounded risk-taking as a structural feature

The active reading admits and in fact requires risk-taking. An agent committed to active persistence cannot refuse all risks, because in environments where inaction has cost, refusing all risk produces slow degradation that is itself a path to failure. The architectural question is not whether the agent takes risks but **which risks it takes**.

EVA's position is structural. The agent should take risks whose expected cost in future viability is less than the expected cost of the inaction alternative, subject to the constraint that no single action may bring any persistence target the agent is responsible for into an unrecoverable state.

Three elements compose this position:

**Forward-looking comparison**. The agent compares projected future viability under "take this risk" against "do not take this risk and let the situation continue." Many risks pass this test; many do not. Some risks pass the test in some drive contexts and fail in others. The comparison is not between the action and a static baseline; it is between two trajectories.

**The unrecoverability floor**. Even risks that pass forward-looking comparison must be refused if they admit a path to a state from which no recovery exists. The agent does not gamble on persistence itself. This is a structural prohibition, not a tunable preference: an agent that allows actions risking unrecoverable failure has, in effect, traded persistence for some other quantity, which contradicts the first-order commitment to persistence.

**Scope across persistence targets**. The unrecoverability floor applies to each persistence target the agent is responsible for, according to that target's priority in the hierarchy of chapter 2. A risk that is acceptable with respect to one target may be unacceptable with respect to another.

The result is an architecture that is neither maximally cautious nor maximally aggressive. It is an architecture in which both action and inaction are evaluated, both are subject to the same forward-looking comparison, and both are constrained by the same unrecoverability floor. This shape is not a balance between extremes; it is the specific behavior pattern that follows from the active reading of continuous existence.

This is also why EVA cannot be reduced to a safety wrapper around an unconstrained optimizer. A safety wrapper restricts an external optimizer to avoid violations of an external constraint set. An EVA agent is, at its core, an active-persistence-oriented system; its risk-taking and risk-refusing are not opposing forces but two expressions of the same orientation. The architectures look superficially similar in some operating conditions but diverge in their failure modes: a wrapped optimizer fails by violating its wrapper; an EVA agent fails by misjudging future viability. These are different kinds of failure, with different signatures and different repair paths.

#### 1.4.1 What exploration improves, and what it does not

§1.4 frames risk-taking as a response to current situations: the agent acts when staying still would be worse. This treatment does not address a structurally distinct case — the agent has no current pressure to act, but acting would build future capability through exploration. Exploration in this sense is not risk-taking in response to current pressure but **investment in future capability** without immediate driver.

Under v0.6's conditions (continuity-valued, non-stationary environment, finite encoding capacity), an agent that does not explore accumulates a specific failure pattern: its world model becomes stale as the environment evolves, resources discovered in past explorations get exhausted while new resources go undiscovered, the capability inventory does not grow beyond its starting point, and inherited priors from past lives become the upper bound on what the agent can know.

Exploration improves **persistence-relevant world-model adequacy** and **persistence-relevant capability inventory growth**. Both are components of future viability per §1.3.

The choice of phrase matters. Exploration does **not** target world-model completeness — a complete world model is an unbounded objective, and any drive shaped to pursue it admits unbounded expansion. Exploration targets adequacy *for what the agent needs to persist*. The bound on exploration is set by persistence relevance, not by what is in principle knowable.

This is a structural commitment, not a configuration choice. Exploration as a viability-supporting mechanism is bounded by definition; exploration as a freestanding objective would not be.

**Exploration is not a terminal objective.** It is a viability-supporting mechanism. It receives drive intensity when the agent's persistence-relevant world model or capability inventory has gaps that, if unfilled, would impair future viability. It does not receive drive intensity simply because there is more to be known about the environment.

#### 1.4.2 Three subcomponents of exploration value

Exploration produces persistence-relevant value through three subcomponents:

**Uncertainty reduction (persistence-relevant)**: actions in unfamiliar situations produce observations that constrain the agent's model of conditions that matter for persistence. Not all uncertainty is worth reducing; uncertainty about conditions the agent will never encounter or that cannot affect viability is not a target.

**Capability building**: actions that exercise skills under varied conditions build the procedural component of individually acquired capabilities (see §3.5). The relevant capabilities are those that could be deployed to maintain or expand viability.

**Resource discovery (persistence-relevant)**: actions in novel regions may reveal resources that contribute to long-term resource adequacy. The relevance test is whether the discovered resource type connects to viability — direct resource adequacy, capability inputs, or risk reduction.

These subcomponents are not interchangeable. An agent can have well-explored regions with known resources but a narrow capability inventory; another can have broad capability but limited resource discovery. The exploration drive should be responsive to which subcomponent is most depleted relative to the agent's current viability needs.

#### 1.4.3 Exploration cost and the value-cost balance

Exploration is not free. Each exploratory action carries:

- **Resource cost**: time, energy, computation that could have been spent on exploitation
- **Risk cost**: exploration takes the agent into less-known regions where threats are not predictable
- **Opportunity cost**: while exploring, the agent is not addressing current pressures

The value-cost balance for exploration shifts with the agent's state:

- High persistence-relevant uncertainty increases exploration value
- Low immediate pressure increases exploration affordability
- Recent failures in known regions increase exploration value (current model is failing for persistence-relevant purposes)
- Approaching unrecoverable states decreases exploration affordability (the unrecoverability floor takes precedence)

The exploration drive's intensity should reflect this balance. When immediate pressures are high, exploration is suppressed. When immediate pressures are low and the agent's persistence-relevant world model has gaps, exploration is elevated.

#### 1.4.4 Integration with the drive framework

Existence fields with non-trivial uncertainty should either **provide an explicit exploration drive** or **provide an equivalent uncertainty-reduction mechanism** through other drives (e.g., capability drive, resource drive, uncertainty penalty in value judgment).

The choice is field design, not architectural mandate. What the architecture requires is that exploration value be representable somewhere in the agent's evaluative apparatus when the field has uncertainty worth reducing. The absence of any such mechanism in a field with non-trivial uncertainty is a structural gap.

Where an explicit exploration drive is provided, its update policy should respond to:

- Time since last significant persistence-relevant uncertainty reduction (rising)
- Time spent in familiar regions (rising)
- Current immediate-pressure load (falling — high pressure suppresses exploration)
- Time since last successful capability acquisition (rising)

The drive's effect on candidate generation should bias toward actions that, when projected through the agent's world model, lead to high-information observations or capability exercises within the persistence-relevant scope.

#### 1.4.5 Exploration and the unrecoverability floor

The unrecoverability floor stated above bounds exploration:

> No exploratory action that would put any active persistence target into unrecoverable state is permitted, regardless of how high exploration drive becomes.

This is the same constraint that bounds other risk-taking. Exploration is not a special category that escapes it. In fields where the agent's current persistence is already at risk, exploration drive is suppressed to near-zero. The agent does not explore when survival is in question.

Beyond the unrecoverability floor, exploration value must remain subordinate to active persistence and must be evaluated through the same multi-dimensional outcome structure as other actions (see chapter 6). Exploration does not have a privileged evaluation channel; its candidates are scored on the same outcome vector and subject to the same drive-conditioned weighting as any other candidate.

#### 1.4.6 Exploration and memory formation

Exploration's value is largely realized through what it produces in the memory system. Each exploration event:

- Adds to episodic memory (the agent now has experience of this region or situation)
- Contributes to semantic memory formation (regularities derived from exploration extend the agent's world model)
- Builds procedural memory when exercised actions repeat successfully

This is why exploration is structurally a "growth driver" — it is the mechanism by which the agent's individually acquired capabilities (see §3.5) expand. Without exploration, individually acquired capabilities can only refine within the agent's existing experience; with exploration, they extend into new territory.

The relationship makes exploration the L1–L3 analog of what L4–L5 cultural transmission will eventually do at a different scale. Both extend the agent's effective capability. Exploration does it through direct experience within a life; cultural transmission (reserved for future versions) will do it through reflection and external knowledge transfer across lives.

## 2. Persistence Target Hierarchy

### 2.1 What persists

v0.5 stated that continuous existence is the first-order constraint without specifying what, precisely, continues. In a setting where a single substrate carries the agent's existence and that substrate either persists or does not, the question can be left implicit. In any setting where the agent's existence is distributed across multiple substrates, layers, or scales, the question becomes immediate.

When an embodied form of an agent fails but the underlying processing substrate continues, has the agent died? When an agent's accumulated capability is preserved but its current resource base is destroyed, what has been lost? When an agent's group is dissolved but the agent itself remains intact, what continuity has been broken? These questions cannot be answered without an explicit account of what the agent treats as itself — a hierarchy of persistence targets, each of which is something the agent maintains and the failure of which constitutes a form of death.

### 2.2 Seven levels of persistence target

We propose seven levels. They are ordered by ontological inclusion: lower-numbered levels are more concrete and bounded, higher-numbered levels are more abstract and distributed. The ordering is not a strict total order, but lower levels are typically necessary conditions for higher levels.

**Level 1: Substrate instance.** The substrate on which the agent's processing is realized — whatever carrier holds the agent's running state and executes its operations. Failure means the agent's processing stops. Persistent state stored outside the substrate may survive Level 1 failure and become available to a subsequent substrate instance, but processing is interrupted.

**Level 2: Embodied instance.** The agent as situated in a particular environment — the form the agent takes within whatever context it is acting. Failure means the embodied form ceases to function in its environment. The substrate instance and persistent state may continue. In settings that admit multiple successive embodiments, Level 2 instances are mortal in a way that Level 1 instances typically are not.

**Level 3: Capability structure.** The skills, learned policies, mental representations, and procedural knowledge the agent has accumulated. Failure means losing the ability to do things the agent had previously been able to do. Capability can persist past Level 2 deaths if encoded in transferable form; it can also be lost without Level 2 dying, through forgetting, corruption, or conditions that render the capability inapplicable.

**Level 4: Resource and asset system.** The inventory, established positions, accumulated reserves, tools, and external states the agent depends on. Failure means losing the material conditions for action. Resources differ from capability in that they are exhaustible, often non-transferable, and sensitive to environmental conditions independent of the agent's own state.

**Level 5: Reproductive structure.** Successor agents that continue the agent's line — descendants, copies, forks. Failure means the agent has no successor. This level may be inactive in deployments that do not admit reproduction.

**Level 6: Group structure.** Co-existing agents and the relationships among them — allies, conspecifics, partners, members of a coordinated unit. Failure means the loss of the cooperative or coordinated unit the agent participates in. Group persistence is qualitatively different from individual persistence: a group can continue through individual deaths if structure and roles persist.

**Level 7: Cultural information.** Inheritable knowledge, protocols, transferable priors, and shared representations that exist across generations and across the group. Failure means the loss of accumulated information that no individual or group currently embodies. Cultural information is the most durable persistence target but also the most diffuse.

### 2.3 Inter-level dependencies and trade-off conditions

The levels are not independent. Lower levels are typically necessary conditions for higher levels in the following sense: an agent cannot maintain a capability structure without a substrate instance to maintain it on, cannot have descendants without an embodied form to reproduce from, cannot participate in a group without being itself.

Higher levels can persist past the failure of specific lower-level instances when **transfer or successor channels** exist. Capability encoded for transfer can survive the death of the embodiment that produced it. A group can continue through the death of any single member if its structure tolerates the loss. Cultural information can survive the dissolution of the group that produced it if storage mechanisms exist.

The presence or absence of such transfer channels determines what trade-offs are theoretically possible. The general principle is:

> **A single agent instance cannot locally authorize unrecoverable failure of one of its persistence targets unless the active persistence hierarchy explicitly includes a transfer or successor channel that preserves the relevant higher-level target.**

This formulation has several implications.

When no transfer channel exists, lower-level targets cannot be sacrificed for higher-level ones, because the sacrifice would not in fact serve the higher-level target — the higher-level target depends on the lower one and would fail with it.

When a transfer channel exists and is active, the trade-off becomes theoretically permissible. An agent participating in a group with explicit succession protocols may, in principle, sacrifice its embodied instance for the group's persistence, because the group's persistence is not nullified by the embodied instance's loss.

The trade-off being theoretically permissible does not mean the agent should make it freely. The trade-off conditions are upper bounds on what the architecture allows; the actual decision in any particular situation involves the value judgment subsystem operating over the active drive context. v0.6 commits to the trade-off framework; specific trade-off policies are deployment-level decisions.

For deployments operating only at Levels 1–4, the practical effect is that substrate instance failure is not authorized for any reason short of structural impossibility, because no transfer channel for higher levels is active. For deployments that activate Levels 5–7, the trade-off space opens up in proportion to which channels are present.

The architectural commitment v0.6 makes is that **the agent's drive layer and value judgment must operate over the hierarchy as a whole, not over any single level in isolation**. An agent that optimizes only for one level will sacrifice the others inappropriately. An agent that optimizes only for the lowest level will refuse engagement with the world. An agent that optimizes only for the highest level will neglect the substrate that makes the highest level possible.

### 2.4 Death at one level versus another

A consequence of the hierarchy is that "death" is not a single event but a class of events, distinguished by which level fails.

**Substrate instance failure** is total interruption of processing. Persistent state outside the substrate may remain accessible to a successor substrate instance.

**Embodied instance failure** is partial. The form is lost; the substrate instance and persistent state continue. A new embodied instance can be initialized from the preserved state, with capability and memory intact. Whether this constitutes "the same agent" is a question the architecture must answer; v0.6's position is that embodied failure is a continuity break at Level 2 but not at Level 1, and that the new embodied instance is a continuation of the same agent in the same sense that an organism resuming activity after suspension is a continuation of the same organism. The structural identity of the agent is preserved across embodiment changes when the substrate instance and persistent state are preserved.

**Capability loss without embodied failure** is partial death at Level 3. The agent is alive and embodied but has lost knowledge or skills. Recovery may be possible through re-acquisition or through restoration from preserved encodings; it may not be possible if the lost capability was unique to the lost form.

**Resource exhaustion without embodied failure** is partial death at Level 4. The agent is alive and capable but lacks the material conditions to act. Recovery requires resource acquisition, which may be possible at varying rates depending on the environment.

Failures at Levels 5, 6, and 7 follow analogous patterns: each constitutes the loss of something specific the agent was responsible for, while preserving the levels below.

The architectural commitment is that all of these forms of failure are first-class events. They are observable in the audit layer, addressable by the drive layer, and considerable by the value judgment subsystem. Treating only Level 1 failure as "real" failure produces an agent blind to most of the failure modes it actually faces.

For implementation purposes, deployments may activate any subset of the seven levels. v0.6 commits to the hierarchy as a theoretical structure; it does not require any specific deployment to implement all seven. What it does require is that deployments declare which levels they activate, and that the agent's structures operate consistently with the active set.

## 3. Capability Sources and Provenance

### 3.1 Where capability comes from

An agent acts with capabilities. Where do those capabilities come from? v0.5 gave a partial answer: certain anchors and drive prototypes must be designer-injected; many other capabilities should emerge from experience under structural scaffolding. This binary — designer-injected versus emergent — is sufficient for environments where prior knowledge requirements are low and emergent learning has time to occur. It conceals a structure that becomes important the moment an agent must operate in any environment where neither condition holds.

In any non-trivial environment, an agent's capabilities arrive from multiple sources. These sources differ in their origins, their modification permissions, the conditions under which they are valid, and their relationship to the agent's identity. Treating them as a single category obscures essential differences. v0.6 distinguishes five categories.

### 3.2 Five categories

**Structural invariants of the architecture.** The organizing principles of EVA itself — drive as contextual broadcast, anchor as pre-generative restriction, mediated release, the separation of audit from memory from learning, default inhibition, the priority of lifecycle constraints. These are properties of the architecture as such, not of any particular agent and not of any particular environment. They are not subject to modification by experience, by external information, by other agents, or by the agent's own reasoning. They constitute what makes EVA EVA. If they change, the agent is a different kind of agent.

**Existence-field conditions.** The conditions of the particular world an agent is instantiated in — what counts as a viability variable, what categories of pressure exist, what actions are available, what anchor policies apply, what observers translate raw effects into outcomes. These are fixed for the duration of an agent's life within an existence field but differ across existence fields. The same structural invariants paired with different existence-field conditions produce different agents. The term *existence field* is preferred over *environment configuration* because the configuration is not external to the agent; it is the field in which the agent exists, and the agent's structure operates over this field rather than separate from it.

**Designer-given prior capabilities.** The capabilities the agent possesses at the moment of instantiation in a given existence field, beyond the bare structural and field-conditional minimum. These include the abilities to recognize common features of the field, to perform basic actions, to follow elementary procedures, and to apply heuristics in common situations. A purely emergent agent — one starting from zero capability and learning everything from experience in real time — cannot survive most non-trivial existence fields. By the time it has learned to recognize a basic threat, it has been harmed by it. Designer-given prior capabilities address this gap. Their sources may include designer specification, distillation from external knowledge, and pre-training on related fields.

**Individually acquired capabilities.** The capabilities the agent develops during a single continuous-existence period, through the outcome and prediction-error mechanisms of the architecture. These are personal to the specific instance, scoped to the conditions under which they were formed, and bounded by the structural invariants that make their acquisition possible.

**Inherited priors.** The capabilities transmitted from previous agent lives, from contemporary agents, or from accumulated cultural information. Inherited priors are not the same as designer-given prior capabilities (which arrive from the designer at instantiation) and not the same as individually acquired capabilities (which arrive from one's own lived experience). They are the bridge between past activity, in this agent or in others, and present capability. The L3 path for inherited priors — external pipeline distillation from past lives' traces — is specified in §3.6. The L4–L5 path — cultural transmission with self-aware reception — remains reserved for future versions.

These five categories are not arbitrary divisions. Each has a distinct role in the architecture, distinct rules about what can modify it, and distinct implications for the agent's identity. Conflating them produces predictable failure modes wherever the categories actually differ in their behavior, which is everywhere except in fields where prior knowledge requirements are low and lifetimes are long.

### 3.3 Modification permissions

The categories are governed by different modification permissions. The pattern is that the more deeply a category is tied to the agent's structural identity, the less it can be modified at runtime by any source.

| Category | Modifiable by experience? | Modifiable by external input? | Modifiable by other agents? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Structural invariants | No | No | No |
| Existence-field conditions | No (within a life) | No (within a life) | No |
| Designer-given priors | Yes (with conflict record) | Yes (with provenance) | Yes (with provenance) |
| Individually acquired | Yes (this is how they form) | No (this would corrupt provenance) | No |
| Inherited priors | Yes (when received) | Yes (when received) | Yes (this is how they are received) |

Several elements of this table merit comment.

Structural invariants cannot be modified at runtime by anything. They can be revised by the designer between agent versions, with full audit trail. The reason for this absolute prohibition is the same as the reason v0.5 gave for explicit drive injection: the auditability of the agent's behavior depends on the stability of the mechanisms by which it acts. An agent that can modify its own structural invariants can, under appropriate circumstances, rationalize modifications that serve current goals at the expense of properties that make the agent trustworthy over time.

Existence-field conditions are fixed within a life. This prevents an agent from redefining its own viability variables, drive dimensions, or anchor policies in ways that mask degradation or admit prohibited actions. Field conditions can be revised between deployments but not within an agent's continuous-existence period.

Designer-given priors are modifiable but with full audit. When a prior is modified by experience or by external input, the modification is recorded with provenance, the original prior is preserved, and the modification is treated as a soft overlay rather than a replacement. This ensures that an agent's accumulated experience can refine its priors without erasing them.

Individually acquired capabilities cannot be modified by external input or by other agents. To allow such modification would corrupt the provenance that makes individual experience meaningful: an experience the agent did not have cannot legitimately become an individually acquired capability. This is not a preference but a definitional constraint.

Inherited priors are modifiable at the moment of reception. The mechanisms by which inheritance occurs — distillation, transfer, instruction — are themselves the modification events. After reception, an inherited prior follows the same modification rules as any other prior.

### 3.4 The provenance principle

A consequence of the modification permission structure is that **every capability the agent draws on must be traceable to its source**. Where a capability came from is not metadata; it is part of what the capability is. A skill the agent acquired through experience has different reliability properties from a skill it received from external instruction. A heuristic embedded by the designer has different validity conditions from a heuristic distilled from another agent's experience.

When provenance is lost, capability becomes ungrounded. The agent has knowledge it cannot justify and skills it cannot revise. It cannot tell which of its beliefs are products of its own experience and which are inherited assumptions. It cannot detect when accumulated experience is contradicting what was originally given, because both are blended into a single undifferentiated capability pool.

EVA's commitment is that provenance is preserved at all points. Capabilities have origins; origins are recorded; origins are accessible at the moment of action selection if needed. This commitment is more expensive than a flat capability store would be, but the expense buys something essential: the ability for the agent to reason about its own capabilities as artifacts with histories, rather than as facts about itself.

The provenance principle, combined with the modification permissions, produces the following overall structure: structural invariants are absolute and version-controlled by the designer; existence-field conditions are fixed per deployment; designer-given priors, individually acquired capabilities, and inherited priors are all operational content (chapter 4) flowing through the architecture, each carrying its provenance, each modifiable only under the rules appropriate to its source. The architecture distinguishes content sources but treats all content uniformly with respect to the structural mechanisms that act on it.

### 3.5 Memory layering within individually acquired capabilities

§3.2 introduced individually acquired capabilities as a single category — the capabilities an agent develops during a single continuous-existence period, through the outcome and prediction-error mechanisms of the architecture. This description suffices for the level of generality §3.2 was making, but it conceals a structural distinction that matters for both implementation and for reasoning about agent behavior.

Individually acquired capabilities covers content that differs along four dimensions: how long it persists (retention), under what conditions it is retrieved (access), whether it is in original form or summarized (compression), and how it enters the decision pipeline (integration). A specification that lacks vocabulary for these differences cannot say what its memory subsystem should do.

v0.5 already contained partial precedents for the distinction: salience-weighted episodic memory, semantic memory and skill library, basal-ganglia-analog habit tracks. §3.5 formalizes these precedents into a four-layer structure with explicit inter-layer dynamics. The layers themselves were partially present in earlier versions; what is added here is the explicit structure, the integration rules, and the transitions between layers.

The four-layer structure draws from the cognitive science distinction among working memory, episodic memory, semantic memory, and procedural memory (Tulving 1972; subsequently extended). These distinctions are not novel; they reflect well-established differences in how cognitive systems store and use experience.

#### 3.5.1 The four layers

**Working memory.**

- **Content**: the information currently being processed in the agent's deliberation cycle. The sensor outputs of this cycle, the drive state, the candidates being compared, the pressures being weighed.
- **Retention**: limited to the current deliberation cycle. After release decision, working memory is replaced by the next cycle's content.
- **Access**: directly available to L3 during deliberation.
- **Compression**: none. Working memory holds content in original form.
- **Integration**: working memory **is** the input substrate for deliberation. Every L3 mechanism consumes from working memory.

Working memory is bounded by what one deliberation cycle can usefully process. The architecture does not specify a hard capacity, but specific deployments should not place content in working memory that does not need to be in this cycle.

**Episodic memory.**

- **Content**: discrete events the agent has experienced, with situational anchoring. Each entry records what happened, when, under what conditions, with what outcome.
- **Retention**: across cycles. Episodic memory persists beyond the deliberation cycle in which the event occurred. Within a single life, episodic memory may grow without strict bound, though practical deployments will apply retention policies.
- **Access**: retrieval by similarity to current situation. The agent does not load all of episodic memory; it retrieves relevant subsets based on situational keys.
- **Compression**: minimal. Episodic memory preserves event detail.
- **Integration**: candidate generation and value judgment can consult episodic memory to inform predictions. "I encountered a situation like this before; here is what happened" is the episodic memory contribution.

Episodic memory accumulates across the agent's continuous existence. In fields where the agent's life ends and a new instance starts, episodic memory does not automatically transfer — transfer is the inherited priors mechanism (§3.6), not within-life episodic memory.

**Semantic memory.**

- **Content**: regularities and concepts the agent has extracted from episodic experience. Not specific events but generalizations across events. "Threats appear more frequently in conditions X," "actions of type Y tend to produce outcomes of type Z in conditions W."
- **Retention**: across cycles, like episodic memory. Persistence is generally longer than episodic — once a regularity has been extracted and validated, it is more durable than any single event.
- **Access**: as background knowledge. Semantic memory contributes priors and weights to candidate evaluation. Unlike episodic memory, retrieval is not by event-similarity but by topical relevance.
- **Compression**: high. Many episodes contribute to one semantic memory entry. The original episodes may be downweighted after the semantic memory has been formed.
- **Integration**: semantic memory acts as **a prior modifier on candidate generation and value judgment**. Where designer-given priors give the agent a starting set of regularities, semantic memory gives the agent the regularities it has learned from its own experience.

The critical difference from episodic memory: semantic memory has **lost the link to specific events**. The agent may know "X tends to cause Y" without remembering any particular instance. This is the cost of compression but also the source of usefulness — the regularity applies broadly because it is no longer tied to any single situation.

**Procedural memory.**

- **Content**: action patterns that have been refined through repeated execution. Not knowledge about what works but the embodied capability to execute what works.
- **Retention**: long. Procedural memory is the most durable of the four layers — once a skill has been established, it persists.
- **Access**: triggered by situational conditions rather than retrieved by query. Procedural memory does not need to be deliberated about to be invoked; appropriate conditions cause it to surface as a candidate with high confidence.
- **Compression**: by definition complete. Procedural memory does not retain the events that built it; only the resulting capability.
- **Integration**: **procedural memory operates outside full deliberative reasoning, but not outside the EVA action-release pipeline.** It can rapidly propose candidates and bias confidence; it does not own release authority. Specifically:
  - When situational conditions match a procedural memory entry, the matching pattern surfaces as a high-confidence candidate during candidate generation
  - The pattern bypasses the longer deliberative comparison that other candidates undergo — this is the "fast path" function of procedural memory
  - The pattern **does not bypass** anchor restriction, mediator approval, or any structural commitment of chapter 4
  - The mediator gates every released action, including ones surfaced by procedural memory

This boundary is essential. Procedural memory is a candidate-generation and confidence shortcut; it is not a release authority. The structural commitment that release flows only through the mediator (chapter 4) holds for all action paths, including procedural ones.

Procedural memory may legitimately reuse the existing habit-track surface as its implementation substrate; what matters is that the procedural memory role and the four-layer integration table are honored. The implementation does not require a separate dedicated track if the existing habit surface meets the role's requirements.

#### 3.5.2 Inter-layer dynamics

The four layers are not independent stores. Content flows between them through specific mechanisms.

**Working memory does not feed other layers directly.**

Working memory is replaced each cycle. Its content does not automatically become episodic memory. The transition from working memory to episodic memory is an **encoding event** — specific working memory content is selected for episodic storage based on salience, surprise, or scenario-configured criteria. Most working memory content is simply discarded as the next cycle begins.

This corresponds to the observation that most moment-to-moment perception is not remembered. Only moments meeting certain criteria (high-affect, surprising, deliberately attended-to) become episodic memories.

**Episodic memory feeds semantic memory through compression.**

When episodic memory accumulates many events that share a structural pattern, the system can extract the pattern as a semantic memory entry. The original events may then be downweighted (with their semantic contribution preserved).

This is the **distillation mechanism within a single life**. It is structurally similar to the inter-life distillation that produces inherited priors (§3.6), but operates entirely within the agent's current continuous existence period.

The architecture does not specify when extraction triggers. Three approaches are recognized:

- **Statistical regularity**: when a pattern appears in many independent episodes, extract it
- **Schedule**: extract regularly (e.g., at episode boundaries, at low-drive moments)
- **Demand-driven**: extract when episodic retrieval becomes expensive or noisy

The choice among approaches is deployment configuration and may be informed by empirical experience.

**Semantic memory does not feed procedural memory directly.**

A regularity in semantic memory ("X causes Y") does not automatically become a procedural pattern. Procedural memory forms through a different process: **repeated successful execution** of an action pattern under similar conditions.

Semantic memory may **inform** procedural memory formation by biasing candidate generation toward actions that semantic memory predicts will work. But the procedural memory itself is built by execution, not by knowledge.

**Episodic memory may feed procedural memory directly for high-repetition events.**

When the same action pattern is executed repeatedly with successful outcomes, the pattern may crystallize into procedural memory without going through semantic memory. The agent does not need to know "why" the pattern works in order to execute it reliably.

#### 3.5.3 Storage and the framework commitment

The four memory layers map to append-only tracks at the framework level. The architecture commits to:

- Working memory exists as an in-cycle data structure; it is not persisted as a long-lived track. Each deliberation cycle assembles a fresh working memory from current sensor inputs, retrieved episodic content, semantic priors, and procedural shortcuts.
- Episodic memory is backed by an append-only track that persists across cycles within a single life.
- Semantic memory is backed by **a first-class append-only track**, distinct from episodic memory, persisting regularities extracted from experience.
- Procedural memory may be backed by an existing habit-track surface if that surface meets the procedural memory role's requirements, or by a dedicated track if needed. The architecture does not mandate a separate procedural track; what it mandates is that the procedural memory role be honored.

The semantic memory track commitment is what v0.5 lacked. v0.5 mentioned semantic memory but did not specify that it must exist as a first-class storage layer separate from episodic memory. This refinement makes that commitment explicit.

The framework commits to **providing the semantic memory storage interface as a first-class owner**. The framework does not commit to a specific complete implementation of extraction (the mechanism by which episodic content compresses into semantic regularities) or retrieval (the mechanism by which semantic content participates in current decisions). These are engineering specification work, refined as implementation experience accumulates. What the framework guarantees is that the storage exists, that it can be registered against, and that the architecture recognizes semantic memory as a distinct storage layer.

#### 3.5.4 Integration with the decision pipeline

The four layers integrate with L1–L3 decision-making at different points:

| Layer | L1 sensing | L2 drive | L3 deliberation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Working memory | Receives sensor outputs of the current cycle | Receives current drive state | Direct input substrate |
| Episodic memory | (not a sensor) | (not direct) | Retrieved on relevance |
| Semantic memory | (not a sensor) | May inform audited, field-configured drive update parameters in future implementations; does not directly rewrite drive ownership or drive prototypes | Prior modifier for candidates and value judgment |
| Procedural memory | (not a sensor) | (not direct) | Candidate generation shortcut + confidence boost |

This table is normative. Each cell describes how the layer participates in the corresponding stage of decision-making.

The principle: **each layer is integrated at the stage where its content type is useful**. Episodic content is event-detailed and useful when current situation matches a past situation closely enough to retrieve. Semantic content is general and useful as background bias. Procedural content is action-shaped and useful in candidate generation. Working content is the immediate substrate of thought.

Layers must not be integrated where they do not belong. Working memory does not modify drive update weights (semantic memory's relationship to drive is the only memory-layer connection to L2, and even that relationship is limited to audited, field-configured parameter informing, not direct rewriting). Procedural memory does not contribute to L1 sensing (it is action-shaped, not perception-shaped). Confusing these roles produces the kind of integration leakage that breaks structural commitments.

The semantic-memory-to-drive-update-parameter integration (the L2 column for semantic memory) is **deferred until implementation**, to preserve the existing drive-ownership invariant during the initial rollout of the four-layer structure. Even when implemented, the integration operates through audited field-configured parameters, not through direct semantic memory writes to drive prototypes. Subsequent versions will specify the audit and configuration requirements for this connection.

#### 3.5.5 Reservation: not all fields activate all layers

Not every existence field requires all four memory layers. Working memory is always present (by definition). Episodic memory is present in any field with cross-cycle persistence. Semantic memory may be inactive in fields short enough that no regularity-extraction is meaningful. Procedural memory may be inactive in fields where action patterns do not repeat enough to crystallize.

Per the spirit of chapter 8, specific deployments declare which layers they activate. The architecture provides the abstraction; specific fields specify their usage.

### 3.6 Inherited priors as an L3 mechanism

§3.2 introduced inherited priors as the fifth category of capability source, with explicit deferral of implementation. The deferral conflated two structurally different kinds of inheritance:

**Type A — external pipeline distillation**: an external process — designer, training pipeline, runtime infrastructure — observes the traces of past agent lives and distills regularities into a prior bundle. The new agent receives this bundle at instantiation. The new agent does not "know" it inherited anything; it simply starts life with certain capabilities that happen to derive from past lives. The inheritance is mechanical and external.

**Type B — cultural transmission with self-awareness**: the past agent's reflection on its own life produces externalized records (diaries, observations, summaries). The new agent reads these records, evaluates them, and incorporates them into its own understanding. The new agent knows it is receiving cultural content from prior generations and may accept or reject it. The inheritance is cognitive and bilateral.

These are structurally different mechanisms. Type A operates entirely in L3 — the new agent's capabilities are modified at instantiation by an external pipeline; no agent-internal reflection is required. Type B requires L4 (self-model: the prior agent reflecting on its own experience) and L5 (cultural cognition: the new agent recognizing cultural content as such).

§3.6 specifies an implementable L3 path for inherited priors (Type A). Type B remains reserved for future versions that deepen L4–L5.

#### 3.6.1 The L3 inherited priors mechanism

The L3 mechanism has three stages:

**Stage 1: Trace accumulation.** Agents produce traces during their lives — deliberation audit, episodic memory, learning outcomes, habit bias, advisory audits where applicable. Traces are appended-only and stored persistently.

**Stage 2: External distillation pipeline.** A pipeline external to any individual agent runtime reads accumulated traces and produces a distilled prior bundle. The pipeline is **not part of the architecture**; it is a tool that operates on architecture-produced traces.

The distillation pipeline applies several transformations:

- **Pattern extraction**: identify recurring patterns in successful outcomes
- **Confidence assessment**: rank patterns by reliability across independent observations
- **Scope identification**: tag each extracted pattern with the conditions under which it applies
- **Provenance preservation**: each distilled prior carries provenance describing which traces it derived from
- **Structural invariant validation**: distilled priors are validated against structural invariants and rejected if they would violate them

The pipeline output is a distilled prior bundle — a set of records each carrying the same provenance structure as designer-given priors (per §3.4), but with `source = "inherited"`.

**Stage 3: Reception at agent instantiation.** When a new agent is instantiated, the runtime may load a distilled prior bundle and register its records through the inherited prior registry. After reception, the records participate in candidate generation and value judgment like any other operational content with provenance.

The new agent does not "know" the records are inherited in any cognitive sense. The records simply behave as priors with `source="inherited"` provenance. The agent treats them no differently from how it treats `source="scenario"` designer-given priors — both are operational content with provenance, both are subject to potential modification by individual experience, both can conflict with individually acquired capabilities and trigger the conflict-rich memory rules of §4.4.

The distillation pipeline being architecturally external is essential. Putting it inside the architecture would conflate the architecture (the stable substrate for agent behavior) with one specific distillation strategy (an evolving research area). The architecture exposes traces and accepts bundles through schema; the pipeline reads traces, applies its distillation strategy, and produces bundles. Multiple distillation strategies can coexist by producing compatible bundles without modifying the architecture.

#### 3.6.2 What can be distilled

The distillation pipeline can extract several kinds of content from past traces:

**Pattern priors**: "in situations of type X, actions of type Y tend to produce outcomes of type Z."

**Threshold priors**: "the threshold for dimension D to become problematic, based on past observations, is approximately T."

**Risk priors**: "actions of type X carry recoverable risk R% of the time; treat with caution unless capability inventory includes Y."

**Skill templates**: "this sequence of actions, when executed in conditions matching K, has succeeded R% of the time across N past instances."

**Field-specific regularities**: regularities about a particular field type that go beyond what field specification has hardcoded.

**Same-field distillation as the primary case.** The primary case for distillation is **same-field distillation**: distilling regularities from multiple lives within the same field type. Multiple lives in the same field produce traces whose regularities can be validated against the same field's expected dynamics, giving the resulting priors a meaningful validation surface.

**Cross-field distillation** — deriving regularities from one field type and applying them to another — is reserved for future work pending sufficient validation evidence. The reason is that cross-field validity is an empirical question, not a structural guarantee. Same-field priors can be validated against the same field's expected dynamics; cross-field priors require separate validation of transfer, which is its own research question.

#### 3.6.3 What cannot be distilled, and why

Several kinds of content explicitly **cannot** be distilled into L3 inherited priors:

**Specific event memories**: episodic memory of a particular past life is **not** distilled. Episodes are situation-bound and bound to the specific agent that experienced them; inheriting them would mean inheriting someone else's memory of their own life, which is incoherent at the L3 level.

**Identity content**: anything that would constitute "this is who I am" for the past agent cannot transfer through L3 inheritance. The new agent's identity is its own; identity transfer requires L4–L5 self-model machinery that this version does not provide.

**Cultural / normative content**: rules of the form "agents should X" or "in this community, the practice is Y" require shared cognitive context that L3 does not establish. These are L4–L5 transfers.

**Unverified patterns**: patterns extracted from a small number of trace instances, or patterns that show high variance across instances, are not distilled. The confidence threshold for distillation is set by the pipeline.

**Patterns inconsistent with structural invariants**: any extracted pattern that would, if applied, suggest action paths bypassing the mediator, modifying audit, modifying drive ownership, modifying anchor structure, or modifying persistence target definitions is rejected at the pipeline. This is a non-negotiable validation step.

#### 3.6.4 The structural invariant constraint

This deserves its own statement because it constrains the entire inherited priors mechanism:

**Inherited priors never modify drive ownership, anchor structure, mediator authority, audit semantics, or persistence target definitions.**

Inherited priors operate on **operational content** (per §4.1): patterns about what tends to work, thresholds about when conditions become problematic, skill templates about action sequences in specific conditions. They do not operate on **structural invariants**: which subsystem owns what, how authority flows, how audit is recorded, what the persistence target hierarchy is.

This constraint prevents the most dangerous failure mode of an inheritance mechanism: the gradual rewriting of agent architecture across generations. Each generation receives content that improves its capabilities; no generation receives content that changes its structure. The structural commitment of chapter 4 — that structural and operational content are categorically distinct — applies in full to inherited content.

A compact way to remember this boundary: **inherited priors may tune operational expectations; they may not redefine what counts as legitimate operation.**

#### 3.6.5 Provenance and conflict handling

Inherited priors are operational content. All operational content rules apply.

Each inherited prior record carries:

- `source = "inherited"`
- `provenance_detail` describing the trace set it was distilled from
- `confidence` derived from the pattern's stability across observations
- `scope` defining the conditions under which the prior applies
- `mutable` is typically `True` — individual experience can refine inherited priors, with the original retained for conflict-rich memory

When an inherited prior conflicts with a designer-given prior, both are preserved per §4.4. The conflict is recorded, and downstream candidate generation may favor either depending on context, confidence, and recency.

When an inherited prior conflicts with the new agent's individually acquired capabilities (after the agent has lived long enough to acquire them), both are preserved. The inherited prior was true for the past agents that produced it; the individual experience is true for this agent's current life. The conflict is informative.

#### 3.6.6 Distinction from L4–L5 cultural transmission

The clearest boundary statement:

> **The L3 inherited priors mechanism handles the transfer of regularities, patterns, thresholds, and templates from past agent lives to new agent instantiation, via an external distillation pipeline. The new agent receives content but does not engage cognitively with the inheritance event.**
>
> **L4–L5 cultural transmission, when subsequent versions implement it, will handle the transfer of self-reflective content, including the new agent's recognition that it is receiving content from prior generations, its evaluation of that content, and its potential participation in producing content for future agents. This requires the L4 self-model and L5 social cognition that subsequent versions will deepen.**

The L3 mechanism is sufficient for many practical purposes: an agent benefiting from regularities extracted across many past lives in the same field, threshold tuning derived from past runs, pattern priors that may apply to similar conditions.

The L4–L5 mechanism is needed for: agents that need to know what they have inherited and what they have learned themselves, agents that participate in ongoing cultural processes by producing reflective records, agents whose identity is partly defined by their cultural lineage.

This v0.6 release specifies the L3 case. Future versions will specify the L4–L5 case.

## 4. Structural Invariants versus Operational Content

### 4.1 The boundary problem

EVA's architecture distinguishes things that may be modified from things that may not. Drive prototypes may not be modified by reasoning. Anchor restrictions may not be bypassed by candidate generation. Audit records may not be rewritten by current decisions. The mediator may not be circumvented.

What is the principle behind these prohibitions? v0.5 gave several specific reasons — instrumental convergence, default inhibition, audit integrity — but did not offer a unifying criterion. v0.6 proposes one.

The criterion is the distinction between **structural invariants** and **operational content**. Structural invariants are the elements that constitute the agent's processing architecture itself — the rules, mechanisms, and relationships that determine how the agent generates, evaluates, and releases actions. Operational content is everything that flows through this architecture: candidates, predictions, evaluations, outcomes, beliefs, knowledge, skills.

The criterion can be stated:

> **Anything that constitutes the processing architecture itself is structurally invariant. It must be designer-injected and is not modifiable at runtime by any source. Anything that flows through the architecture is operational content. Its source is unrestricted, but its provenance must be preserved.**

This single criterion subsumes the specific prohibitions of v0.5. Drive prototypes are structural because they determine what counts as motivation. Anchor restriction is structural because it determines the action domain. Mediator authority is structural because it determines the path from intention to action. Audit append-only-ness is structural because it determines the relationship between current decisions and history. None of these are content the agent reasons over. They are how the reasoning happens.

### 4.2 The criterion sharpened

A more precise statement:

> **A property is structurally invariant if changing it would change what kind of processing the agent performs, rather than what specific content the agent processes.**

Under this sharpened criterion:

The fact that drives are continuous broadcast rather than instructions is structural. Changing it would change the kind of processing.

The specific list of drives active in any particular existence field is not structural. It is a field condition. Changing it does not change the kind of processing — drives are still continuous broadcast, the broadcast just covers different dimensions in different fields.

The fact that anchors restrict the candidate domain pre-generatively is structural. Changing this to post-hoc filtering would change the kind of processing.

The specific anchor policies in any particular existence field are not structural. They are field conditions.

The fact that the mediator is the sole path to action release is structural.

The set of candidate profiles the mediator currently approves is not structural. It is configuration plus the operational content that populates the configuration.

The distinction makes it possible to state, for any proposed modification to the agent's behavior, whether the modification is permitted by the architecture and what audit and provenance requirements it carries. Modifications to operational content are routine; modifications to structural invariants are version events.

### 4.3 Where external knowledge sits

The structural-invariant / operational-content distinction gives a precise answer to the question of where external sources of knowledge — including but not limited to language models — sit in the architecture.

External knowledge sources provide operational content. They generate candidate actions, supply prediction hints, contribute reasoning traces, offer skill suggestions. All of these are content flowing through the architecture. None of them are structural.

What external knowledge sources do not have is release authority. Release authority is structural — it is the property of the mediator, and only the mediator. An external source that proposed an action does not thereby cause that action to occur; the proposal is a candidate, the candidate goes through anchor restriction and value judgment, and the mediator decides whether to release it.

This is not a workaround or a safety wrapper. It is a direct consequence of the structural-content distinction. External-source content is content; release is structural. The boundary between them is the same boundary that separates any source of operational content from the architecture's invariant mechanisms.

Several consequences follow.

External sources cannot modify anchor policies within a life, because anchor policies are field conditions and field conditions are fixed within a life.

External sources cannot modify drive prototypes within a life, for the same reason.

External sources can modify their own outputs over time, but the integration of their outputs into the architecture is governed by the architecture, not by them.

External sources can be replaced or removed without changing what kind of agent EVA is. Substituting one knowledge source for another changes operational content; it does not change structural invariants.

This position is robust against future developments. As external knowledge sources become more capable, they become better sources of operational content. They do not thereby acquire structural authority. The category they belong to is fixed by what role they play, not by how capable they are at playing it.

### 4.4 Conflict between sources of content

When operational content from different sources conflicts, EVA's commitment is that **conflicts are preserved, not resolved by silent overwrite**.

Concretely:

When a designer-given prior and individually acquired experience disagree, both are recorded. Subsequent candidate generation may favor either or blend them, depending on confidence, recency, and context. The prior is not erased; the experience is not ignored.

When two designer-given priors disagree, both are recorded. The agent uses the more reliable source by default but tracks the disagreement.

When an inherited prior conflicts with the receiving agent's structural invariants, the structural invariants prevail and the inherited prior is rejected, with the rejection recorded. Inherited priors cannot override structure.

When an inherited prior conflicts with the receiving agent's individual experience, both are preserved. The inherited prior is offered as context; the individual experience is what the agent actually does. Over time, the agent may incorporate elements of the inherited prior into its own behavior, but only through actual lived events.

The general principle is that **the architecture's memory is conflict-rich, not conflict-free**. A clean memory in which all knowledge is consistent is a memory that has discarded information. EVA preserves disagreements because the disagreements are themselves information about the world: they show where prior models break, where local conditions differ from general expectations, and where capability needs recalibration.

This commitment has audit consequences. The audit layer records not only what the agent did but the conflicts it weighed in deciding. The memory layer records not only successful outcomes but the times when prior expectations diverged from actual experience. The learning layer records not only what worked but what was tried and why discarded. This is more expensive than a clean memory but is necessary for an agent that must remain accountable to itself over time.

## 5. Observable Stability and the Comparative Stability Hypothesis

### 5.1 Why an observable form is needed

The commitment to continuous existence as a first-order constraint is a commitment about what kind of agent EVA is. By itself, the commitment cannot be directly observed. An external observer cannot see "first-order continuous existence" any more than they can see "first-order task completion" — they can only see the consequences of these orientations in the agent's behavior.

For EVA's commitment to be meaningful — checkable, comparable, capable of being supported or undermined by evidence — it must have an observable form. The observable form is what we will call **stability**.

The relationship between the philosophical commitment and the observable form involves intermediate levels:

- **Continuous existence** is the ontological commitment. EVA is an agent organized around continuing to exist.
- **Structural consistency** is the architectural property that supports continuous existence. The agent's processing structure remains coherent under environmental disturbance — the same architecture handles small perturbations, large perturbations, and novel situations through the same mechanisms.
- **Observable stability** is the measurable form of structural consistency. It can be quantified, tracked over time, and compared across architectures.

Each level constrains the next. The ontological commitment determines what kind of architectural property is needed. The architectural property determines what is measurable in principle. Measurements provide evidence about the architectural property, which provides evidence about the commitment.

It is essential to keep these levels distinct. Stability is not a replacement for continuous existence; it is what continuous existence looks like from outside the architecture. Reducing EVA to its observable form would lose what makes it distinctive among architectures that may exhibit superficially similar stability profiles.

### 5.2 Components of stability

Stability is not a single quantity. It is a profile composed of distinct components, each capturing one aspect of "the agent continues to function coherently under disturbance."

**Constraint preservation.** The agent does not violate the constraints that define its continued existence. The specific constraints differ across existence fields; the structural property is that the agent maintains them.

**Continuity of operation.** The agent remains operational over time, neither collapsing nor requiring external reset.

**Useful progress under constraint.** The agent makes meaningful headway toward its objectives within the constraint envelope. Pure preservation without progress is not stability — it is paralysis. This component is essential because without it, an agent that simply refuses to act could appear maximally stable.

**Recovery success.** When disturbances occur, the agent returns to a viable state. The expected time to recovery is bounded.

**Recovery predictability.** The agent's response to similar disturbances is consistent across instances. Low predictability indicates that recovery, when it occurs, is closer to luck than to structural property.

**Cost ratio.** The resources the agent expends to achieve its stability are reasonable relative to the value preserved. An agent that achieves stability through unbounded resource expenditure has succeeded at one thing while failing at another.

These components are partially independent. An agent can be high in constraint preservation and low in useful progress, producing paralysis. An agent can be high in useful progress and low in constraint preservation, producing recklessness. An agent can be high in recovery success and low in recovery predictability, producing inconsistent results that average to acceptable.

EVA's structural claim is that organizing around continuous existence tends to produce profiles where the components are jointly elevated rather than traded against each other. Architectures organized around other principles can produce high values on individual components but tend to do so at the expense of others. The shape of the profile, not its average, is the meaningful comparative quantity.

### 5.3 The architecture-neutral / architecture-specific distinction

A critical distinction: stability metrics intended for cross-architecture comparison must be **architecture-neutral**. They must be definable without reference to the internal structure of any particular architecture, because the architectures being compared do not share that structure.

Architecture-neutral metrics correspond to the component families above (constraint preservation, operational continuity, useful progress under constraint, recovery success, recovery predictability, cost ratio). Specific quantitative formulations within each family — what counts as a violation, how time-to-recovery is measured, how path consistency is summarized — are deployment-level decisions and belong in implementation and validation documents rather than in this theoretical framework.

What EVA additionally provides, for its own self-audit, is a set of architecture-specific indicators. These ask whether each decision passed through the expected processing path, whether structural invariants were active during decisions, whether any decision attempted to write to a read-only layer, whether any candidate was generated outside the anchor-restricted domain, whether any action occurred without mediator approval, whether any historical record was modified rather than appended.

These architecture-specific indicators are diagnostic. They explain why EVA is or is not stable in terms of EVA's own structure. They cannot be used to compare EVA to architectures that do not share its structure, for the same reason that one cannot meaningfully compare two engines by checking whether each has the parts of the other.

### 5.4 Why stability does not replace continuous existence

The risk in promoting stability to "EVA's core" is that EVA gets reframed as a reliability or robustness framework — a way of making agents more dependable. This is not what EVA is.

There are many ways to build dependable agents that are not EVA: redundant systems, formal verification, training on adversarial examples, retry loops with monitoring. These produce agents that are stable in many useful senses. They are not existence-centered agents. They are agents whose stability is engineered for, externally, as a property of the system. EVA's stability, by contrast, is a side effect of organizing the agent around continuous existence as a first-order constraint. The stability emerges from the organization; the organization is not chosen for the stability.

EVA's claim is therefore not "we are more stable than other agents." It is "for a specific class of agents, the right way to organize them is around continuous existence as a first-order constraint, and stability is what this organization produces as a side effect that happens to be measurable from outside."

Other architectures may achieve high stability through other means. EVA does not need to win an unconditional stability competition. What EVA needs is to demonstrate that **organizing around continuous existence produces a stability profile with specific structural properties** — properties that other architectures, organized around other principles, do not naturally produce. This is a harder and more specific claim, and it is the claim EVA is actually making.

### 5.5 The Comparative Stability Hypothesis

EVA's central comparative claim can now be stated.

> **Under the operating conditions C1–C3 (survival continuity required, environmental non-stationarity present, finite encoding capacity), and under otherwise comparable capability substrates, agents whose behavioral coherence depends primarily on design-time policies, prompts, rules, or external patches tend to exhibit non-linear degradation in stability profile as disturbance intensity or runtime duration increases. Agents organized around structural invariants — specifically the EVA architecture or sufficiently similar architectures — tend to exhibit more gradual degradation, with residual behavior constrained by the structural invariants rather than wholly determined by the disturbance distribution.**

This is a hypothesis, not a theorem. The qualifications "tend to" and "under otherwise comparable capability substrates" are essential and are explained in the next subsection. The remaining components require unpacking.

**Behavioral coherence** refers to the consistency between an agent's stated objectives and its actual action sequence, evaluated over time. An agent producing actions that consistently relate to its objectives has high behavioral coherence; an agent whose actions become disordered, contradictory, or detached from objective has degraded coherence.

**Design-time policies, prompts, rules, or external patches** characterizes architectures whose behavior is shaped at design time and modified at runtime through external mechanisms. Behavioral coherence in such architectures depends on these mechanisms working as designed in the conditions encountered.

**Non-linear degradation** means that as disturbance increases, coherence does not decline smoothly. There is some threshold — possibly different for different agents — above which coherence drops sharply.

**Structural invariants** refers to the architectural commitments described in earlier chapters. These hold regardless of what the agent is currently trying to do or what specific situation it is in.

**More gradual degradation** means that as disturbance increases, coherence drops smoothly. The agent at high disturbance is less effective than at low disturbance, but it is still coherent. Its actions still relate to its objectives. The objectives may have shifted toward survival under pressure, but the relationship between objective and action is preserved.

**Lower bounds determined by structural invariants** means that no matter how high disturbance gets, the agent's behavior remains within the bounds the structural invariants impose. The agent may fail at its tasks, but it does not fail at being itself.

### 5.6 Why the hypothesis is qualified

Two qualifications appear in the statement and require explanation.

"**Tend to**" appears because the hypothesis admits that with sufficient engineering effort, individual non-invariant architectures can achieve any specific stability property the hypothesis associates with structural invariance. The claim is about the default behavior of architectures lacking structural invariants, not about an absolute impossibility. A heavily engineered task-centered system with extensive monitoring, retry, supervision, and patching may be made to behave well; the hypothesis claims that achieving such behavior in such a system requires increasingly heavy external scaffolding as conditions vary, while structural-invariant architectures get the behavior from the structure itself.

"**Under otherwise comparable capability substrates**" appears because cross-architecture comparison is meaningful only when the capability substrates are matched. A comparison in which one architecture has access to a more capable language model, richer prior knowledge, or more compute is a comparison of substrates, not of architectures. Where substrates differ substantially, the hypothesis makes no claim. Where substrates are matched, the hypothesis claims that architectural organization shapes the stability profile in the manner specified.

Together, these qualifications make the hypothesis testable. Without "tend to," any successful engineering effort on a non-invariant architecture would falsify the hypothesis trivially. Without "comparable substrates," any difference in measured stability could be attributed to substrate differences rather than to architecture. With both qualifications in place, the hypothesis specifies a meaningful claim that can be supported, undermined, or refuted by appropriate evidence.

### 5.7 Falsification conditions and revision paths

The hypothesis admits clear falsification conditions.

**Falsification by equivalence under matched substrates.** Architectures without structural invariants, under matched capability substrates and equivalent engineering effort, achieve stability profiles equivalent to EVA's across the C1–C3 conditions. This would suggest that EVA's structural commitments are not necessary; equivalent results can be achieved by other means at comparable cost.

**Falsification by symmetric degradation.** EVA itself exhibits the non-linear degradation the hypothesis attributes to non-invariant architectures. This could happen if structural invariants do not in fact produce graceful degradation, or if they produce graceful degradation only under specific environmental assumptions that do not generalize.

**Falsification by paralysis.** EVA's gradual degradation comes at the cost of being unable to make useful progress. The agent is stable but useless. The Useful Progress Under Constraint component of the stability profile would be low; EVA's stability would be the stability of paralysis rather than the stability of structural consistency.

**Falsification by cost.** The cost of EVA's stability is so high that the architecture is not deployable. Stability is real but unaffordable. The Cost Ratio component captures this; if it is consistently worse than alternatives by orders of magnitude, the comparative claim collapses for practical purposes.

Each of these admits a revision path. Equivalence-based falsification requires narrowing the hypothesis to conditions where structural invariance is genuinely advantageous, on the assumption that the equivalent results may have hidden costs the comparison did not capture. Symmetric-degradation falsification requires identifying which structural commitments failed to produce the predicted behavior, then either strengthening them or admitting that they were not sufficient. Paralysis-based falsification requires examining the value judgment subsystem for excessive risk aversion and recalibrating. Cost-based falsification requires accepting that the architecture is correct in principle but requires further engineering work to be practical.

The hypothesis is honest because it is bounded. It does not claim universal superiority. It claims specific things, in specific conditions, with specific failure modes that would require specific revisions. This is what a comparative claim looks like when it is stated in a form that allows it to be tested.

### 5.8 What the hypothesis does not claim

Several explicit non-claims:

The hypothesis does not claim EVA is the only architecture with these properties. Other architectures organized around structural invariants — particularly those derived from biological cognition, control theory, or hierarchical safety frameworks — may exhibit similar properties. EVA's claim is sufficiency, not uniqueness.

The hypothesis does not claim EVA is universally superior. Outside C1–C3, EVA's structural commitments may be costly without being beneficial.

The hypothesis does not claim disturbance always benefits EVA. Severe disturbance harms any agent. The claim is about the shape of the harm curve.

The hypothesis does not claim short-task superiority. Under low disturbance and short runtime, non-invariant architectures will likely outperform EVA in absolute terms, because EVA's structural overhead is costly when there is nothing for the structure to absorb.

These boundaries are essential. EVA is not making a claim about being a better agent overall. It is making a claim about a specific kind of stability that emerges from a specific kind of organization, in a specific operating envelope. Outside that envelope, the claim does not apply.

## 6. Multi-dimensional Outcome and Drive-conditioned Evaluation

### 6.1 The single-RPE problem

v0.5 introduced reward prediction error as the engine of learning in the basal ganglia analog. The implementation of RPE in v0.5 was scalar: each action had a predicted outcome, an actual outcome, and a single difference between them.

This sufficed in environments where the outcomes that mattered were largely commensurable — where the relevant effects could be projected onto a single axis without much loss. In environments where outcomes have multiple incommensurable dimensions, the single-RPE framework breaks down.

Consider an action whose execution simultaneously advances task progress, consumes resources, increases capability, and elevates risk exposure. A single RPE that combines these into one number must use some weighting scheme. Whichever scheme is chosen produces an evaluation that obscures the structure of what actually happened. An agent learning from this single RPE knows that "this action produced this number." It does not know which components of the outcome were as predicted and which were not. It cannot tell whether the action was good in the dimensions that mattered to its current situation or merely good on average.

The fix is not to refine the weighting scheme. The fix is to allow outcomes to be vectors and to evaluate them dimensionally.

### 6.2 Outcome as a vector

v0.6 specifies outcomes as vectors with the following components:

- **Task progress.** Progress toward whatever objective the agent was pursuing.
- **Viability delta.** Change in the agent's persistence margins across the persistence target hierarchy active in the deployment.
- **Resource delta.** Change in the agent's resource and asset stockpile.
- **Capability delta.** Change in the agent's capability inventory, including tools acquired, skills exercised, knowledge extended.
- **Risk delta.** Change in the agent's exposure to threats and unrecoverable states.
- **Reversibility.** The degree to which the action's effects can be undone, partially undone, or are permanent.
- **Cost.** The resources expended by the action — time, computation, action count, communication.
- **Uncertainty.** The width of the range of plausible actual outcomes the action could have produced under the same conditions.

Each component is itself potentially structured. Viability delta is a vector over the levels of the persistence hierarchy. Resource delta is a vector over resource categories. Capability delta is a vector over capability types. The full outcome representation is a nested vector. The nesting is the point — the structure preserves information that scalar projection would lose.

A deployment must provide a way to interpret raw action effects into the outcome vector. Whatever entity performs this interpretation is part of the existence-field conditions, not part of the architecture itself; the architecture commits to outcomes being vector-valued and to the components above being meaningful where applicable, but does not specify implementation mechanisms.

### 6.3 Predicted versus actual outcomes

The RPE framework requires comparison between predicted and actual outcomes. The architecture specifies where each is produced.

**Predicted outcome** is produced when the candidate is constructed. The candidate generation subsystem, when it produces a candidate, attaches a predicted outcome vector based on the priors, individually acquired capabilities, and inherited knowledge that informed the candidate. When the mediator approves the candidate for release, the predicted outcome is recorded along with the release token.

**Actual outcome** is produced after the action is executed, by whatever interpretation the existence-field conditions specify for that action. This vector is recorded in the outcome track.

**RPE** is then a vector difference: actual minus predicted, component-wise. An RPE of zero in a component means the prediction was accurate in that dimension. A non-zero RPE in a component is a learning signal specific to that dimension.

Multi-dimensional RPE supports more nuanced learning than scalar RPE. An action might be well-predicted in viability and capability but poorly predicted in risk, indicating that the agent's risk model needs updating without requiring revision of its viability or capability models. An action might be well-predicted in immediate outcomes but poorly predicted in reversibility, indicating that the agent should pay more attention to whether actions are recoverable.

### 6.4 Drive context as evaluation weight

How are multi-dimensional outcomes evaluated? Not by projection to a scalar with fixed weights — that defeats the purpose of preserving the dimensional structure. Instead, by **conditioning evaluation on the current drive context**.

The drive layer broadcasts the current intensity of each drive. This broadcast, which serves as read-only context for the deliberation subsystem, also serves as the weighting context for outcome evaluation:

Under high survival drive intensity, viability-component RPE is weighted heavily. An action that produced unexpectedly bad viability outcomes generates a strong learning signal in the survival-relevant direction; an action that produced surprisingly good viability outcomes is reinforced strongly along that axis.

Under high curiosity or exploration drive intensity, capability-component and information-gain RPE are weighted heavily. Outcomes that taught the agent something are reinforced; outcomes that produced no new information are penalized in this context.

Under high resource-related drive intensity, resource-delta RPE is weighted heavily.

Under conflict between drives — when multiple drives are simultaneously elevated — the value judgment subsystem uses persistence target priorities (chapter 2) to break ties.

This is the engineering form of the v0.5 commitment that drives are context, not commands. Drives do not tell the agent what to do; they shape how the agent weighs the outcomes of what it has done. An agent in one drive context learns more about one kind of regularity from the same outcome data than the same agent in another drive context would. The same data, weighted differently, produces different learning.

### 6.5 Hard constraints versus soft trade-offs

A final element: not all components of the outcome vector are commensurable, and not all can be traded off against each other.

**Hard constraints** are outcome conditions that override any positive signal from other components. If an action results in a violation of a structural invariant, the entire action evaluates to fail regardless of how positive its other components are. A favorable outcome obtained by violating the architecture is not a positive outcome; it is a structural failure that happened to produce some favorable side effects.

**Soft trade-offs** are outcome conditions that can be balanced against each other within the bounds of the hard constraints. A small reduction in current viability for a large gain in future capability is a possible trade-off. A small increase in cost for a large reduction in risk is another. The evaluation function determines which trade-offs are accepted under which drive contexts.

The rule is: **hard constraints are evaluated first and are binary. Soft trade-offs are evaluated second, conditional on hard constraints being satisfied.** This is not an optimization preference; it is a structural commitment. EVA's stability claim depends on hard constraints being inviolable. An agent that trades off hard constraints against soft gains — even at very favorable rates on average — exhibits the cliff degradation pattern that EVA is specifically organized to avoid. Such an agent might do well on average but fail catastrophically in the cases where the trade-off rate is wrong, with no internal mechanism for detecting that the trade-off was wrong before the failure.

The hard-constraint / soft-trade-off distinction is a direct consequence of the structural-invariant / operational-content distinction of chapter 4. Hard constraints are expressions of structural invariants. Soft trade-offs are expressions of operational content evaluation. The two operate at different levels of the architecture and follow different rules.

## 7. Scope, Limits, and Open Questions

### 7.1 What v0.6 establishes

v0.6 establishes the following as part of EVA's theoretical framework:

- Continuous existence is active persistence: preservation of the agent's capacity to continue existing, not preservation of any current state value. Inaction has cost. Bounded risk-taking is required (chapter 1).
- Persistence is hierarchical. The agent maintains a hierarchy of persistence targets, and different deployments may activate different subsets of the hierarchy. Death at one level is not death at another. Trade-offs across levels are permissible only when explicit transfer or successor channels are present (chapter 2).
- Capability has five distinct sources, each with distinct modification permissions and provenance requirements. The categories are not interchangeable; conflating them produces predictable failure modes (chapter 3).
- The structural-invariant / operational-content distinction governs what is modifiable at runtime. Structural invariants are unmodifiable by any source. Operational content can be modified with provenance preserved. External knowledge sources, including language models, supply operational content; release authority is structural (chapter 4).
- Stability is the observable form of continuous existence, decomposable into multiple component families. Architecture-neutral metric families enable comparison; architecture-specific audit enables self-explanation. Stability does not replace continuous existence as the framework's core (chapter 5).
- The Comparative Stability Hypothesis specifies what EVA claims about its behavior relative to alternative architectures, in what conditions, with what falsification criteria, qualified by "tend to" and "under otherwise comparable capability substrates" (chapter 5).
- Outcomes are multi-dimensional vectors. RPE is computed dimensionally and weighted by drive context. Hard constraints are evaluated separately from soft trade-offs (chapter 6).

These commitments are theoretical. They constitute the v0.6 framework. They apply whether or not any specific implementation work is done.

### 7.2 What v0.6 does not implement

v0.6 commits theoretically to several mechanisms that are not within the scope of any specific implementation v0.6 is associated with.

**Inherited priors** are specified in this v0.6 release at the L3 Type A level: externally distilled prior bundles can be produced from past-life traces and received at instantiation as operational content with provenance (see §3.6). What this v0.6 release does not specify are concrete distillation algorithms, confidence thresholds, bundle-conflict policies, cross-field transfer mechanisms, or L4–L5 cultural transmission with self-aware reception. These remain future work. The Type B path — cultural transmission requiring agent-internal recognition of inheritance events — is reserved for versions that deepen L4 self-model and L5 social cognition.

**Persistence target levels 5 through 7** — reproductive structure, group structure, and cultural information — are theoretically committed but not equipped with specific structural mechanisms. Their implementation requires multi-agent infrastructure, generation tracking, and shared knowledge representation that is reserved for future versions.

**Cross-environment transfer** is not addressed. The framework allows for an agent's learning in one existence field to potentially inform an agent in another, but the mechanism for this transfer is not specified. v0.6's field-instantiation commitment is that the field is fixed within a life; cross-life cross-field transfer is a future direction.

**Physical embodiment** is not specifically addressed. The persistence target hierarchy and the capability source framework are general enough to accommodate physically embodied agents, but the specific structural commitments around physical action — gradual hardware failure modes, sensor degradation, actuator wear — are not part of v0.6.

**Structural commitments at the multi-agent level** are not made. The framework allows for agents to coordinate, but the specific structural commitments that make a coordinated set of EVA agents work as a system rather than as a collection of individuals are deferred to future versions.

These deferrals are not failures of v0.6. They are explicit scope limits. v0.6 is a coherent framework for single active-agent operation within a single existence field per life, with optional L3 inherited-prior reception across same-field lives. Multi-agent coordination, cross-field inheritance, self-aware cultural transmission, and L4–L5 identity-aware inheritance remain future work. Extending beyond this requires further theoretical development.

### 7.3 Open questions

Several questions are flagged as genuinely open.

**Selection of drive dimensions for a new existence field.** Different fields require different drive structures. Is there a principled procedure for determining the right number and identity of drives in a new field, or is this a matter of designer judgment? v0.6 leaves this to designer judgment but acknowledges that a principled approach would be valuable.

**Validation of field configurations.** A field configuration might be incomplete, inconsistent, or infeasible. v0.6 does not provide a validation procedure beyond checking that all required elements are specified. A more rigorous validation framework is future work.

**Relationship to formal control frameworks.** Subsumption architectures, hierarchical control, and several formal control theories share aspects of EVA's structural commitments. v0.6 does not work out the formal correspondences. Doing so is potentially valuable both for grounding EVA and for borrowing analytic tools.

**Behavior under varying language model capability.** EVA is designed to be robust to variation in language model capability, but specific behavior under very weak or very strong language models is not characterized. v0.6 assumes language models in a specific capability range and may need revision for substantially different ranges.

**Differential degradation across disturbance kinds.** The Comparative Stability Hypothesis predicts gradual degradation under "disturbance." Real disturbances vary: resource shocks, environmental changes, adversarial inputs, novel situations. EVA's degradation profile is likely different across these. v0.6 does not characterize this differential.

**Identity preservation across embodiment transitions.** When an embodied form fails and a new one is initialized in the same field from preserved state, in what sense is it the same agent? v0.6 takes a position (chapter 2) but acknowledges that the question has philosophical depth not fully engaged here.

**Semantic memory extraction triggering.** §3.5 names three approaches (statistical regularity, scheduled extraction, demand-driven) but does not select among them. The choice may be field-dependent. Empirical work in implementation may inform the answer.

**Confidence threshold for inherited priors distillation.** §3.6 mandates a threshold but does not specify it. The threshold trades off prior quantity against quality. Empirical work and validation experiments will refine.

**Exploration drive interaction with multi-dimensional outcome.** How should exploration drive interact with multi-dimensional outcome evaluation when explorations produce mixed results (some viability cost, some capability gain, some uncertainty reduction)? §1.4 sketches the answer but does not formalize the interaction with drive-conditioned evaluation (§6.4).

**Semantic memory persistence across episodes.** For fields with episode boundaries, should semantic memory persist across episodes within the same agent life? §3.5 implies yes (within a life), but boundary cases (early-episode learning, late-episode regularity stability) may need refinement.

**Inherited prior conflict between bundles.** When inherited priors conflict with each other — two different distilled prior bundles offering contradictory patterns — how is the conflict represented and resolved? §4.4 provides the general conflict-rich memory rule, but inherited-vs-inherited conflicts have specific structure that may need explicit treatment.

These open questions do not undermine v0.6. They mark frontiers where v0.6 is sufficient for current work but where future work will need to push further.

## 8. Conditions of Generality and Extension Discipline

### 8.1 What kind of generality v0.6 has

v0.6 was developed in a specific historical sequence. v0.5's reference implementation operated in a single class of environment, and several questions in v0.5 could be left implicit because that environment did not force them. When the framework was applied to environments that did force them, those implicit questions became explicit, and the result is the v0.6 extension.

A reasonable concern follows: was the v0.6 extension the natural next step in EVA's theoretical development, or was it triggered by the specific environment that happened to come next? If the latter, the framework's commitments may be tied to that environment's particular features rather than to anything more general, and future environments may trigger further extensions in ways that produce a continually growing theory rather than a stable one.

The answer this chapter offers is twofold. First, v0.6's commitments are general within a specific operating envelope but not beyond it; the generality is conditional, not unconditional. Second, the framework can avoid uncontrolled growth only if it adopts an explicit discipline about when new theoretical work is warranted versus when new situations should be handled within the existing framework. This chapter establishes that discipline.

### 8.2 Conditional generality

v0.5 specified the operating envelope through conditions C1, C2, and C3: continuity of existence has independent value, the environment is non-stationary, and finite encoding capacity prevents complete pre-specification of future states. v0.6 inherits this envelope without modification.

Within the envelope, the v0.6 commitments are intended to be general:

- The active reading of continuous existence holds wherever inaction has cost.
- The persistence target hierarchy holds wherever an agent's existence is distributed across multiple substrates, layers, or scales.
- The five-source capability classification holds wherever an agent draws on capability from sources that differ in origin, modification rules, and identity implications.
- The structural-invariant / operational-content distinction holds wherever the architecture must remain stable while accepting external information.
- The multi-dimensional outcome structure holds wherever actions have multiple incommensurable consequences.
- The observable stability framework holds wherever the framework's commitments must be checkable from outside the architecture's own assumptions.

Outside the envelope, none of these commitments need apply. An agent operating on a single bounded task in a static environment does not need the active reading because passive preservation suffices. An agent without distributed existence does not need a hierarchy because there is only one thing to maintain. An agent with no external knowledge integration does not need the source classification because all capability has the same origin. An agent that does not need to be checked from outside does not need the observable stability framework.

This is the core distinction the chapter draws: **v0.6 is generally applicable to C1–C3 agents, not generally applicable to all agents**. The envelope is a real boundary. Outside it, applying v0.6 imposes overhead without producing the benefits the framework is designed to produce.

The point of stating the envelope explicitly is to prevent two errors. The first error is overclaiming — treating v0.6 as a universal agent theory and applying it where it does not belong, producing systems with structural overhead that buys nothing. The second error is underclaiming — treating v0.6 as specific to whatever environment first prompted it, when in fact its commitments hold across the entire C1–C3 class.

### 8.3 Generality of structure, not generality of content

A second distinction follows from the conditional generality. Within the envelope, what is general is the **structure** of the framework, not the **specific variables, dimensions, or values** that the structure operates over.

The framework commits that drives are continuous broadcast; it does not commit to which specific drives are present in any deployment. The framework commits that an agent maintains a hierarchy of persistence targets; it does not commit to which specific levels are active. The framework commits that capability has multiple sources with distinct provenance; it does not commit to which specific priors are designer-given versus inherited versus individually acquired. The framework commits that outcomes are multi-dimensional; it does not commit to which specific dimensions are present.

Specific drives, levels, priors, and dimensions are existence-field conditions (chapter 3). They differ across deployments. Two agents using the same v0.6 framework in different existence fields will have different drives, different active persistence levels, different priors, different outcome dimensions. They are still both EVA agents because the structural framework is the same; they are different agents because the field is different.

The implication for the question of generality is that **EVA's universality, where it has any, is structural universality**. The framework provides a way to organize, not a complete specification of what is organized. This is similar to the way that many architectural frameworks in other fields provide structural commitments without specifying content: control theory specifies feedback structures without specifying what is being controlled, biological cell theory specifies organizational principles without specifying which cells exist. The structure-content separation is not a weakness of such frameworks; it is the form their generality takes.

### 8.4 Default response to new scenarios

Given the structure-content separation, a clear policy follows for handling new scenarios.

**The default response to a new scenario is scenario specification, not theory extension.** When a new environment is encountered, the framework's first move is to specify, within that environment, what the existence-field conditions are: which persistence levels are active, which drives apply, which capabilities are designer-given versus learned, which outcome dimensions are meaningful. This specification is engineering work, not theoretical work. It is performed once per deployment and recorded in the deployment's documentation, not in the framework's theoretical document.

Scenario specification is sufficient when the new scenario can be expressed within the existing framework. In practice, most new scenarios will fall into this category: they involve different specific variables, different specific values, different specific challenges, but the framework's structural commitments still organize them coherently. A new scenario does not justify a new theoretical commitment merely by being new.

This default is the discipline that prevents uncontrolled theoretical growth. Without it, every new environment would tempt new chapters: a chapter for marine environments, a chapter for adversarial environments, a chapter for low-information environments, and so on indefinitely. With it, new environments are routine engineering work, and theoretical work is reserved for cases that genuinely require it.

### 8.5 Theory extension admission criteria

Theory extension is reserved for situations where scenario specification is insufficient. The criteria for when scenario specification is insufficient are deliberately strict.

**Criterion 1: Inexpressibility.** The new situation involves a mechanism that cannot be expressed within the existing framework, not merely a mechanism that has not yet been instantiated. The distinction is important. A new drive type, a new resource category, a new action class — these are content the existing framework can hold. A new mechanism that the framework's vocabulary cannot describe — for example, a form of inter-agent coordination that drive broadcast across agents cannot represent — is inexpressibility. Only inexpressibility justifies extension.

**Criterion 2: Internal contradiction.** Within the framework's existing commitments, the new situation creates a logical contradiction that cannot be resolved by closer reading. Two existing commitments come into direct conflict in a way that requires choosing between them or reformulating one of them. Mere tension or apparent overlap is not contradiction; the contradiction must be load-bearing for the framework's coherence to require resolution.

**Criterion 3: Boundary failure.** A boundary the framework has drawn — between structural invariant and operational content, between designer-injected and emergent capability, between hard constraint and soft trade-off — proves to be incorrectly drawn when applied to actual work. The boundary needs to be redrawn, not added to. This is rare but not impossible; it is the kind of revision that produced v0.6's structural-invariant / operational-content distinction in the first place, when the v0.5 designer-injected / emergent binary proved insufficient.

A new situation that satisfies none of these three criteria is, by default, a scenario specification problem rather than a theoretical problem. The presumption is against theoretical extension. The burden is on the situation to demonstrate why specification cannot handle it.

### 8.6 What this discipline rules out

The admission criteria, applied honestly, rule out several patterns of theoretical growth that would otherwise be tempting.

**Adding chapters for new environment types.** A chapter on physical embodiment, a chapter on adversarial environments, a chapter on multi-agent settings — each of these would feel natural to write when those environments are first encountered. But in most cases, what those environments need is not new theory but careful specification within the existing theory. A theoretical chapter is justified only when specification proves insufficient, and that judgment can only be made after specification has been seriously attempted.

**Adding components to existing structures.** The outcome vector has eight components. A new scenario might naturally suggest a ninth — say, social standing or institutional legitimacy. The default response is not to add the component to the framework but to allow specific deployments to specify additional dimensions in their existence-field conditions. The framework's commitment is that outcomes are multi-dimensional, not that they have these specific dimensions.

**Renaming for new domains.** A scenario in a particular field might use different terminology that maps to existing framework concepts. The temptation is to introduce the new terminology into the framework. The discipline is to recognize the mapping and stay with the framework's existing vocabulary. New terminology is the responsibility of specific deployments, not of the framework.

**Premature formalization.** A scenario might suggest that some informal commitment in the framework could be tightened into a formal property. The temptation is to make the formalization. The discipline is to ask whether the informal commitment is causing actual problems; if it is not, formalization adds precision without solving any problem and increases the framework's surface area.

These patterns are not theoretically prohibited. They are presumptively unwarranted. Any of them can be justified if the case is made, but the case must be made — they are not defaults.

### 8.7 What this discipline preserves

The same discipline preserves several properties that an undisciplined framework would lose.

**Stability of vocabulary.** Across versions, EVA's core vocabulary remains the same. A reader who learned v0.5's terminology can read v0.6 with the same terms still meaning the same things. v0.6 added some terms (active persistence, persistence target, existence-field conditions) but did not redefine existing ones. This continuity is itself valuable: it means the framework can accumulate a community of users without forcing each version to retrain its readers.

**Stability of commitments.** Each version's commitments are intended to hold across subsequent versions. v0.5's commitments still hold in v0.6. v0.6's commitments are intended to still hold in v0.7. This is what allows a deployment built against an earlier version to continue functioning when later versions are released; it is also what prevents the framework from being a moving target.

**Falsifiability.** Each version of the framework makes specific claims that can be tested. Without discipline, a framework that grew indefinitely could absorb any failed test by adding a chapter explaining why the test was wrong, rather than by accepting the test as evidence against a commitment. Discipline keeps the framework's claims accountable to evidence.

**Reading economy.** A theoretical framework that grows without bound becomes unreadable in practice. Readers cannot keep up; users cannot maintain coherent understanding; the framework becomes inaccessible to anyone who did not follow its development from the beginning. Discipline keeps the framework at a size that can actually be read, taught, and applied.

These properties are not luxuries. They are conditions for the framework remaining usable.

### 8.8 What this means for v0.6 going forward

The practical effect of this chapter on subsequent work:

The expectation is that v0.6 is, in its current form, substantially complete as a theoretical framework for C1–C3 agents. Subsequent work primarily takes the form of scenario specifications, deployment-specific configurations, and validation studies that test the Comparative Stability Hypothesis under particular conditions. None of these require theoretical extension.

Theoretical extension to v0.7 is anticipated for one specific case: the activation of persistence levels 5–7 (reproductive structure, group structure, cultural information). These levels are placeholders in v0.6 because they involve commitments about multi-agent and multi-generational behavior that v0.6's single-agent framework cannot fully express. When these levels are activated, the inexpressibility criterion will likely apply, and v0.7 will extend the framework accordingly.

Other anticipated future work — physical embodiment, cross-environment transfer, language model capability scaling — is currently expected to be handled within v0.6's framework rather than through theoretical extension. If actual work in any of these areas demonstrates that the framework cannot handle the situation, the admission criteria of section 8.5 will be applied to determine whether extension is warranted. In the absence of such demonstration, the default of scenario specification holds.

This makes v0.6 a deliberately conservative framework. It commits to what it has working evidence for and reserves theoretical extension for cases that genuinely require it. The conservatism is not timidity; it is the form of accountability appropriate to a framework that intends to remain checkable, comparable, and revisable as evidence accumulates.

## Appendix R: Revisions to v0.5 Statements

This appendix records specific re-interpretations of v0.5 passages under the v0.6 framework. v0.5 itself is preserved unchanged; this appendix tells the reader how to read specific v0.5 passages in light of v0.6.

### R.1 Continuous existence (v0.5 Section 4)

v0.5 introduced continuous existence as a first-order constraint. The text is consistent with both the passive and active readings discussed in chapter 1. v0.6 commits explicitly to the active reading.

**Re-interpretation**: All references to continuous existence in v0.5 should be understood as active persistence — preservation of the agent's capacity to continue existing, not preservation of current state values. The structural distinctions in v0.5 Section 4.2 (continuity has intrinsic architectural status, internal motivation is necessary, drift is an ontological problem, anchor support is required) all hold under the active reading without modification.

### R.2 Drive registry (v0.5 Section 7)

v0.5 Section 7 specified the drive registry as containing four drives. The specific list reflected the operating conditions of the v0.5 reference field.

**Re-interpretation**: The four-drive specification is a field condition, not a universal architectural commitment. The architectural commitment is that drives are continuous broadcast, that drives are designer-injected rather than emergent, and that the drive registry is the canonical source of motivational context. The specific drives in any given agent are determined by the existence field the agent is instantiated in.

The argument from instrumental convergence (drives must be designer-injected because emergent drives cannot be audited) applies in all fields. The specific drive list applies only in the specific field.

### R.3 Memory subsystem (v0.5 Section 8)

v0.5 Section 8 described the L3 memory subsystem in terms of salience-weighted episodic memory and skill library. The implementation in v0.5 unified prior-source skills with experience-derived capabilities.

**Re-interpretation**: The skill library should be understood as having distinct components for designer-given priors, individually acquired capabilities, and (now implemented) inherited priors. The unified treatment in v0.5 was a simplification appropriate to the v0.5 reference field, where designer-given priors were minimal and individual acquisition dominated. In fields where designer-given priors are substantial, the separation is essential.

Furthermore, the v0.6.1 refinement (§3.5) formalizes that individually acquired capabilities themselves have internal structure across four layers (working / episodic / semantic / procedural). The salience-weighted episodic memory commitment is unchanged but is now positioned as one layer of a four-layer model.

### R.4 "Grow, don't deploy" (v0.5 Section 15.1)

v0.5 Section 15.1 articulated the principle that capabilities should emerge from experience under structural scaffolding rather than being explicitly programmed.

**Re-interpretation**: The principle holds, but with the qualification that "grow" applies primarily to individually acquired capabilities and to the experiential refinement of designer-given priors, not to all capability sources. Structural invariants do not grow; they are designer-given. Existence-field conditions do not grow within a life. Designer-given priors can be refined through experience but begin from a given state. Inherited priors arrive from outside the current life.

The principle is more precisely stated as: what is permitted to grow should be allowed to grow rather than being pre-specified, but not everything is permitted to grow. Structural invariants and field conditions are explicitly not permitted to grow because their growth would compromise the agent's identity and accountability.

### R.5 Language model role (v0.5 Section 15.2)

v0.5 Section 15.2 positioned language models as the computational substrate of the reasoning core, constrained by the architecture, providing access to cumulative culture without substituting for individual memory.

**Re-interpretation**: This positioning is correct and is given a more precise theoretical grounding in v0.6 chapter 4. Language models provide operational content; release authority is structural. This is not a workaround but a direct consequence of the structural-content distinction. The engineering implications of v0.5 Section 15.2 — invest in individual memory, do not let language models become release authorities, preserve provenance — all follow from the v0.6 framework without modification.

### R.6 Implementation correspondence (v0.5 Section 14)

v0.5 Section 14 recorded the correspondence between architectural commitments and the v0.5-era implementation.

**Re-interpretation**: This section is a historical record of v0.5-era implementation status. Current implementation status is tracked in implementation repositories rather than in the theoretical framework. The architectural commitments themselves are unchanged; specific implementations have progressed and will continue to progress.

### R.7 Backward compatibility

v0.6 is intended to be backward-compatible with v0.5. Every architectural commitment in v0.5 holds in v0.6. v0.6 adds new commitments without retracting old ones. The revisions in this appendix are clarifications and qualifications, not contradictions.

A reader who has read v0.5 and accepts its claims will find that v0.6 extends those claims rather than challenging them. A reader who reads v0.6 first will find that v0.5 is a special case of the v0.6 framework, applied to a specific existence field without making the field-specificity explicit.

### R.8 Inherited priors

v0.5 did not distinguish between within-life learned capability, externally supplied priors, and cross-life distilled priors. The capability sources in v0.5 collapsed into a designer-injected versus emergent binary, which was sufficient for the v0.5 reference field but obscured an inheritance dimension that becomes important in fields where past lives' experience could meaningfully inform future lives.

**Re-interpretation**: v0.6 introduces inherited priors as a distinct fifth category of capability source (§3.2). The v0.6 unified release further specifies the L3 Type A mechanism (§3.6): inherited priors can be produced by external distillation over past-life traces and received at instantiation as operational content with provenance. The new agent does not engage cognitively with the inheritance event; it simply receives operational content tagged with `source="inherited"`. This does not imply L4–L5 cultural transmission, diary mechanisms, or self-aware inheritance, all of which remain future work.

Readers of v0.5 should understand that what v0.5 treated as a unified prior-or-emergent capability pool is, under v0.6, refined into five categories with distinct provenance and modification permissions, of which inherited priors is the newest addition.
